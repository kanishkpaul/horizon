# Horizon: a local-first decision OS

**Horizon should be an open-source, local-first decision OS: infrastructure for
understanding possibilities, making feasible commitments, and learning how to
act as the world changes.**

Every person acts with limited resources, incomplete knowledge, and competing
priorities. Each action changes the world, reveals information, and opens or
closes future possibilities. Other people respond with their own intentions and
constraints. Good decisions therefore require continuity between what we know,
what we value, what we expect, what we commit to, and what subsequently happens.
Horizon should maintain that continuity.

Its fundamental object is a living decision case. A case connects evidence,
assumptions, alternatives, resources, policies, commitments, and observations.
It preserves why a choice made sense at a particular moment and identifies when
its foundations change. The system should help answer: What can I do? What
matters to this choice? What would change my mind? What should I learn before
committing? What remains possible afterward?

**`hdb` provides the evolving account of reality on which decisions depend.**
It should preserve statements alongside their sources, interpretations, temporal
validity, applicability, conflicts, and revision history. Evidence, assumptions,
model suggestions, and observations retain distinct meanings. Relationships
between records make support and dependence inspectable; revisions expose
affected conclusions while preserving historical reasoning.

Embeddings give this evidence system associative reach. People should find
relevant knowledge without knowing its exact vocabulary or location. Semantic
retrieval can connect an unresolved question to a document, an analogous
experience, or a neglected observation. Structured queries preserve exact
constraints; lexical search preserves precise references; dependency traversal
reveals support and contradiction. Together, these methods should retrieve
evidence according to both meaning and the requirements of a decision. Learned
representations remain replaceable views over preserved records.

**`hsim` provides disciplined exploration of what actions could produce.**
It should evaluate adaptive policies across explicit models of resources,
mechanisms, uncertainty, and information. A policy specifies how to act as
observations arrive. Its consequences include effort, costs, outcomes, learning,
dependencies, and remaining options. Probabilities, alternative scenarios,
bounds, and unresolved mechanisms should retain their appropriate interpretations.

Game theory extends this exploration into a social world. Collaborators,
employers, institutions, competitors, and communities respond to incentives and
expectations. Their behaviour can change when we change our actions. Horizon
should support reasoning about cooperation, bargaining, signalling, competition,
and shared resources through explicit, revisable behavioural models. It should
also examine feedback: advice that many people follow can change the conditions
that made that advice attractive.

The application connects these independent libraries. It assembles reviewed
evidence into immutable cases, presents conditional comparisons, and helps users
turn chosen policies into executable commitments. Simulation and execution must
agree on deadlines, resource consumption, prerequisites, and information
availability. Actual outcomes then become observations for review. Changed
knowledge should update current reasoning without rewriting earlier expectations
or erasing completed work.

The frontier is making sophisticated reasoning useful under ordinary limits of
attention, data, and computation. Horizon should refine models where disagreement
could change a commitment, surface consequential unknowns, and help users stop
analysing when further work has little practical value. Its foundations come
from provenance, information retrieval, decision theory, causal modelling,
control, and game theory. Building them into a coherent, inspectable learning
loop creates opportunities to extend those foundations through use.

Public benefit requires accessible tools, portable records, optional models,
and explicit human priorities. Decisions should expose consequences for affected
people as well as the person operating the system. Users should retain ownership,
understanding, and authority.

**Horizon's thesis is that people can exercise greater agency when knowledge,
possibility, commitment, and experience remain connected—and when the tools
supporting that connection belong to everyone.**
